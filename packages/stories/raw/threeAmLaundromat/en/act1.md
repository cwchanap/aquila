# Act 1: The Same Dryer

**Narrator**: 3:07 a.m.

**Narrator**: One of the fluorescent tubes in the laundromat was dying. Every few seconds it twitched, like someone on a night shift nodding off. The rest of the tubes were diligent, bleaching every machine a hospital white.

**Narrator**: Outside, it was raining. The rain crawled down the whole front window and smeared the white light of the convenience store across the street into a blur, like a moon that refused to sleep, seen through frosted glass.

**Narrator**: When Zhou Yining pushed the door open, he was already there.

**Yining**: (thinking) Thursday. Of course.

**Narrator**: He sat in the second orange plastic chair along the wall. Always the second one. A stack of printed pages lay open on his knees, a red pen in his hand, its cap clamped between his teeth. Beside him, Dryer No. 4 glowed warm amber, turning.

**Narrator**: No. 4 was the only machine in the place that actually dried anything.

**Narrator**: No. 1 sounded like a plane taking off. No. 2 took three rounds of ten-dollar coins, and your clothes still came out warm, damp, and faintly aggrieved. Nos. 3 and 5 wore OUT OF ORDER signs so yellowed they looked like historical artifacts.

**Yining**: (thinking) No. 4 might be the only thing on this street that clocks out on time. More on time than me.

**Narrator**: She set her laundry bag down in front of Washer No. 3. No. 3 was hers. No. 1 leaked, and No. 2 smelled of someone else's fabric softener, fragrant to the point of hostility.

**Narrator**: Coins, detergent, start. The rush of water filled the room and drowned out the rain.

**Narrator**: Then she sat in the fourth chair, one empty seat away from him, and pretended to look at her phone.

**Narrator**: They had never exchanged names.

**Narrator**: It had been about six months. Every Thursday she finished her evening shift, handed over, wrote up her notes, rode her scooter home, showered, discovered she couldn't sleep, and carried a week of scrubs down here. He was always here. Always a little earlier than her, as if this place were his office.

**Narrator**: Everything she knew about him came from observation. An occupational hazard.

**Narrator**: Twenty-eight, give or take. Thin-framed glasses. The same grey cardigan every week, with a small hole in the cuff where a pen had gone through. His dark circles were roughly on par with her patients on post-op day two.

**Narrator**: When he worked through his pages, he read them under his breath. One sentence, one stroke of the pen, then the sentence again. Sometimes Japanese, sometimes Chinese, sometimes neither, just him arguing with a sentence.

**Yining**: (thinking) A translator. Or a very diligent lunatic.

**Narrator**: The English half of the instructions on the wall used to say "Please not put the animal inside." After one Thursday, someone had neatly corrected it in red ink to "Do not put pets in the machines," and added a line in small print underneath: "Or people."

**Yining**: (thinking) I have no proof. But only one person in here carries a red pen.

**Narrator**: No. 4 dinged.

**Narrator**: He took the cap out of his mouth, opened the dryer, and pulled his clothes out. He touched them, paused for half a second, then stuffed the whole lot into his bag anyway.

**Red Pen**: No. 4. It's yours.

**Yining**: I've still got twenty-something minutes. And yours aren't dry.

**Red Pen**: They can stand to be a little damper. They don't mind.

**Yining**: You said that last week.

**Red Pen**: They didn't mind last week either.

**Narrator**: It was the longest conversation they had ever had. The record had been set last month. Tonight they tied it.

**Yining**: (thinking) ...Six months, and I still don't know how to answer that.

**Narrator**: A scooter hissed by outside, throwing up a sheet of water. Its headlight slid across the glass like a very slow shooting star.
