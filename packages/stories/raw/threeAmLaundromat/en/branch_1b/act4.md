# Act 4: Left on the Table

**Narrator**: She refolded the note along its original creases. Quarters, edges aligned, like clipping a handover sheet back onto the clipboard at the nurses' station.

**Narrator**: Back into the zip bag, back into the grey sock, and onto the dead center of the folding table, square with the edge.

**Yining**: (thinking) This is the most polite option. Not keeping it, not throwing it away. Property returned, no comment.

**Yining**: (thinking) And I'm tired. I handed over fourteen beds tonight. I don't have the energy to like someone at four in the morning.

**Narrator**: She stuffed the dry clothes into her bag and pushed out the door.

**Narrator**: The rain had eased. Her scooter was parked at the far end of the arcade, the seat soaked. She wiped it twice with her sleeve, couldn't get it dry, and sat down on it anyway.

**Narrator**: Before she started the engine, she didn't look across the street.

**Narrator**: She was quite sure the back over there hadn't turned around either.

**Narrator**: On the ride home, the sky began to grey. Every metal shutter on the street was still down. Only the breakfast shop was lit, steam pouring off its bamboo steamers, as if the city were letting out a very long yawn.

**Yining**: (thinking) Next Thursday, then. He'll be in the second chair, I'll be in the fourth. Business as usual.

**Yining**: (thinking) Business as usual is fine.

**Narrator**: The next Thursday, the second chair was empty.

**Narrator**: The grey sock was gone from the folding table. She didn't know whether he'd taken it, or some early-rising auntie had taken pity on it.

**Narrator**: The Thursday after that, empty too.

**Narrator**: And the one after that.

**Yining**: (thinking) Good. Very polite. Message received.

**Narrator**: At the end of the month the new roster came out, and she was moved to day shifts. The laundromat by day was another world: kids, aunties, someone taking a call on speakerphone. No. 4 was always in use.

**Narrator**: She never came at three in the morning again.

**Narrator**: Four months later, she rotated back onto evenings.

**Narrator**: The first Thursday, she finished her shift, showered, lay wide awake until 2:50, then got up and carried a week of scrubs out the door.

**Narrator**: It was raining again. The white light across the street was still a blur. The dying tube was still flickering, as if no one had touched it in four months.

**Narrator**: The second chair was empty.

**Yining**: (thinking) Of course.

**Narrator**: She put her clothes in No. 3 and, out of habit, walked over to No. 4.

**Narrator**: There was a sheet of paper taped to its door.

**Narrator**: "OUT OF ORDER. Has been eating everything lately."

**Narrator**: Red ink. Neat, typeset-looking handwriting.

**Narrator**: She stood in front of it for a long time. Five years of reading doctors' handwriting; recognizing one person's was not hard. The paper was still white, the edges of the tape not yet curling. It hadn't been put up four months ago.

**Yining**: (thinking) He still comes. On some Thursday. At some three a.m. when I'm not here.

**Narrator**: At the very bottom of the sheet was one more line, so small it seemed afraid of taking up room.

**Narrator**: "Still looking for the other one."

**Narrator**: Anyone else would assume some unlucky soul had lost a sock.

**Narrator**: She took the pen from her pocket and put the cap between her teeth. This time, she didn't take it out.

**Narrator**: She didn't write anything. Not yet.

**Narrator**: She walked over to No. 2 and dropped in the first ten-dollar coin. Two more to go.

**Yining**: (thinking) Next Thursday. Come a little earlier.
